da vinci and the perfect man

— the measure of the body —

Venice · c. 1490 · pen and ink on paper

the perfect man

Around 1490, Leonardo took a sheet of paper and drew a naked man twice over: arms straight out and feet together inside a square, then arms raised and legs spread inside a circle. Above and below it he wrote his notes, the way he wrote everything, backwards, right to left: the measure of man.

The idea wasn't his. Fifteen hundred years earlier, the Roman architect Vitruvius had written that a temple should be built with the same proportions as a well-made human body, and that such a body, arms and legs outstretched, fits exactly into both a circle and a square. Plenty of Renaissance artists tried to draw it. Most gave up or made the man look stretched. Leonardo solved it with one change: he stopped trying to centre both shapes on the same point.

The circle is centred on the navel, the square on the groin. Two centres, one body.

what he wrote around it

The notes under the drawing are a list of ratios, each part of the body a fixed fraction of the whole. Taken together, they are Leonardo's recipe for the "perfect" man:

  • =the outspread arms equal the height
  • ¼the shoulders, at their widest
  • ⅛below the chin to the top of the head
  • ⅙above the chest to the top of the head
  • ¼the nipples to the top of the head
  • ⅒the hairline to the bottom of the chin
  • ⅕the elbow to the tip of the hand
  • ⅒the whole hand
  • ⅐the foot
  • ½the genitals mark the middle of the man
  • ¼the sole of the foot to below the knee
  • ⅓chin to nose, nose to eyebrows, eyebrows to hairline: each a third of the face
Florence · Milan · Rome · c. 1487–1513

under the skin

The proportions told him what the body looked like from outside. That wasn't enough. To paint a body that moved, Leonardo wanted to know what was moving it, so he went inside.

By his own count he dissected about thirty human bodies, working at night in hospital mortuaries in Florence, then Milan and Rome, sketching quickly before the corpses decayed. For a while around 1510 he worked with Marcantonio della Torre, a young anatomy professor at Pavia, and the two planned a full treatise on the human body. Della Torre died of plague in 1511, and the book was never finished.

He invented much of how anatomy is still drawn: the cutaway, the exploded view, the cross-section, and above all the habit of showing the same part from several sides, turned around on the page as if you were walking around it. The plates below follow that habit: one body, seen from before and from behind.

By the ancients man has been called the world in miniature; and certainly this name is well bestowed, because, inasmuch as man is composed of earth, water, air and fire, his body resembles that of the earth. — Leonardo, in his notebooks

None of it was published in his lifetime. His notebooks passed to his pupil Francesco Melzi and were then scattered; the anatomical sheets ended up in the Royal Collection at Windsor Castle. Anatomists didn't really see them until the late 1800s, by which time medicine had found most of it again on its own.

      so what makes him perfect?

      a symmetrical outline, a lopsided inside

      Look at the two plates again. The outline is mirror-perfect, just as Vitruvius promised. The inside isn't. The heart leans left. The liver fills the right. The right lung has three lobes, the left only two, to leave room for the heart. The right kidney sits lower than the left, pushed down by the liver. The stomach and spleen crowd to one side, the appendix hangs off the other.

      Leonardo spent years measuring the perfect proportions of the outside, then cut it open and found that nothing inside obeys them, and that this is exactly how it works. The "perfect man" was never the symmetrical one. He's the one whose parts each fit where they're needed.

      That's a useful thing to remember about a mind, too.
